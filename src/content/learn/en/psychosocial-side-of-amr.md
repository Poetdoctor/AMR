---
title: "The psychosocial side of AMR: what providers are seeing"
summary: >-
  We interviewed six clinicians and researchers separately. They disagreed about
  plenty, and converged on one thing: the psychosocial burden is real, and it is
  overlooked.
order: 3
---

Between the science and the stewardship data there is a category of harm that
almost nothing measures. We asked six people who see AMR from different angles —
a microbiologist, infectious disease physicians, an infection control nurse, a
health researcher — what they were seeing. They were interviewed separately.
Four themes came back from all of them.

## 1. AMR has a communication problem

Not a public-relations problem. A plain-language one.

Dr. Bob Hancock’s account is that the public does not understand why resistance
is serious, because antibiotics still appear to work most of the time. Dr.
Richard Lester sees the result at the bedside: patients misunderstand what
resistance is, and often think that they themselves have become resistant. Dr.
Edith Blondel-Hill finds patients frequently do not understand their diagnosis,
their screening, or why particular precautions are being taken.

Dr. Anthony Liu offered the most uncomfortable version of the explanation: the
more specialised a physician becomes, the further they get from the basics of a
condition — and the less they naturally debrief a patient on them. Nobody
decides to stop explaining. It just stops being anybody’s job.

Liu and Lesley Kooner both extended this to nurses, who spend the most time with
patients and are therefore best placed to explain any of it, if they are
supported to.

## 2. Patient education is part of care, not an extra

Every person we interviewed said some version of this. Hancock would lead with
the consequences of losing antibiotics rather than with statistics. Lester wants
accessible resources and better provider–patient communication built into
training. Blondel-Hill wants screening, isolation and results explained as a
matter of course. Kooner’s observation was simply that many patients have very
little understanding of antibiotics or resistance, and that this is fixable.

## 3. AMR is not only a medical problem

This is where the interviews were most consistent, and where the existing data
is thinnest.

Lester listed what he sees: anxiety and uncertainty, stigma, fear of
transmitting the infection to family and friends, and a sense of personal
responsibility for having developed a resistant infection in the first place.
For people facing long-term resistant infections, those burdens run for months
or years alongside repeated procedures and drug side effects — and often go
unaddressed precisely because the illness itself is severe enough to crowd them
out. Pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing or
low income all compound it.

Blondel-Hill described isolation, separation from partners and newborns,
invasive screening, stigma, and reduced contact with providers. Kooner described
the mechanics that produce it: fewer visitors, less staff interaction, more PPE
between the patient and every human being they see.

The shared conclusion, in the team’s notes from those interviews, is one line:
psychosocial burden is important and overlooked.

## 4. Patients need a stronger voice in AMR

Lester: providers should listen to patient concerns and explain treatment.
Blondel-Hill: emphasise empathy. Kooner: patients should be able to take a lead
in their own care. Dr. Mroz went furthest and recommended engaging people with
lived experience directly, rather than designing around them.

Liu named the obstacle honestly. Healthcare settings are extremely busy, and
there is a culture in which patients do not want to bring things up. If you wait
for patients to raise the psychosocial side unprompted, in a rushed appointment,
you will mostly not hear about it.

## What this adds up to

There is a serious gap between how AMR is understood medically and how it is
experienced. The clinical picture is a bacterium and a drug. The lived picture
is uncertainty, isolation, stigma, repeated screening, conversations that did
not happen, and treatment that has to be fitted around a life.

Addressing resistance needs better antimicrobials and better stewardship. On the
evidence of these interviews, it also needs accessible education,
patient-centred communication, and a good deal more empathy than the system
currently has time for.
