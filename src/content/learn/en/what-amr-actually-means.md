---
title: What antimicrobial resistance actually means
summary: >-
  The single most common misunderstanding about a resistant infection is which
  side of it became resistant. It isn’t you.
order: 1
---

If you have just been told you have an antimicrobial-resistant infection, there
is a good chance nobody had time to explain what that sentence means. That is
the most common thing patients told us, and the most common thing the clinicians
we interviewed said they wished they did better.

So, plainly:

> Reinforce that bacteria are resistant — not the patient.
>
> — Dr. Edith Blondel-Hill, infectious disease physician

## You have not become resistant to antibiotics

The bacteria have. Antibiotics work by interfering with something bacteria need
to survive. Bacteria reproduce quickly and in enormous numbers, and every so
often one of them happens to carry a change that blunts that interference. When
antibiotics are around, that one survives while the others do not — and its
descendants inherit the trait. That is resistance. It is a property of a
bacterial population, not of a person’s body.

The distinction sounds academic until you notice what the other version implies.
“I am resistant to antibiotics” suggests something about you — something you
did, or something now permanently wrong with you. It isn’t true, and it is the
starting point for a lot of the shame patients described to us.

## “Superbug” does more harm than it looks

The word tends to arrive from outside the clinic — from the news, from a
relative. One patient heard it from her own daughter:

> Mom, remember when we were kids and you were reading us all about the fact of
> overuse of antibiotics and [that] we’re gonna create a superbug? Mom, this is
> what you got — a superbug!

People hear “superbug” and reasonably conclude that no antibiotic will work at
all. That is usually not the case. Clinicians often still have options; what
resistance does is make the list of options shorter and the treatment more
complicated — longer courses, different drugs, more monitoring, sometimes
intravenous rather than oral.

Shorter list is a genuinely serious problem. It is a different problem from no
list at all, and the difference matters a great deal when you are the person
lying awake thinking about it.

## Why this is so widely misunderstood

Dr. Bob Hancock, who researches this at UBC, gave us the clearest explanation of
why public understanding lags so far behind: for most people, most of the time,
antibiotics still work. Prescriptions still clear infections. Clinicians still
have fallback options. The problem is real and growing, but from the outside it
is largely invisible until it reaches you personally.

That invisibility is also why AMR is usually explained with statistics, and why
statistics land so poorly. What is actually at stake is the floor that a lot of
modern medicine stands on. Routine surgery, transplants, cancer treatment,
newborn care — all of it assumes that if an infection turns up, something will
treat it.

## What this means for you, practically

Nothing here is a treatment plan; your clinician has that. But knowing the
mechanism changes what you can ask for, and patients who ask tend to get told
more:

- What is the name of the organism, and what is it resistant to?
- Which options were considered, and why was this one chosen?
- What side effects should I expect, and what should make me call someone?
- How long is this course, and how will we know whether it worked?

The clinicians we interviewed were unanimous that these questions are welcome,
and that a rushed appointment is not the same thing as an unwelcome one.
