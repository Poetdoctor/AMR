Antimicrobial resistance can affect anyone. It does not affect everyone equally,
and the pattern is not random.

## What raises the risk

Dr. Bob Hancock pointed us to two groups that come up repeatedly and get
discussed rarely.

**Women.** Urinary tract infections are common, frequently treated on a
pharmacy-led pathway, and often not followed up. Repeated courses of antibiotics
without anyone tracking the pattern across them is a reliable way to select for
resistance.

**People with catheters,** who are frequently also wheelchair users. A
long-term indwelling device is a route in for bacteria, and the infections that
follow tend to be recurrent, which again means repeated treatment.

Dr. Richard Lester added the factors that make the burden heavier once an
infection is resistant rather than making it more likely in the first place:
pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing, and
lower socioeconomic status. Each of these makes a long treatment course harder
to complete and a psychosocial burden harder to absorb.

## Where the data is thin

Most of what is known about AMR in British Columbia comes from hospital samples.
That is genuinely good data — nineteen years of it — but it is collected from
people who reached a hospital and stayed long enough to be recorded. It
systematically under-counts the people least likely to end up in a hospital
record at all.

Our own literature review kept landing on the same list of groups: people in
long-term care, people experiencing homelessness, refugees, Indigenous
communities living with underfunded water and housing infrastructure, cancer
patients, newborns, and the healthcare workers alongside all of them.

## A note on how risk gets talked about

Dr. Edith Blondel-Hill raised something worth repeating carefully.

Different parts of the world do have different rates of resistance and different
bacterial strains, and travel and healthcare abroad are legitimate clinical
questions. But the way that question gets asked matters enormously. Asked
badly, a reasonable screening question becomes an accusation, and a patient
hears that they are being treated as a source of contamination because of where
they are from.

Blondel-Hill also noted the predictable consequence of getting this wrong: some
patients avoid screening altogether once they learn they have a risk factor for
AMR. Stigma does not reduce transmission. It reduces the number of people who
come forward to be tested.

## Why this section exists at all

If resistance concentrates in the groups that were already underserved, then
counting prescriptions and resistant samples will describe the problem
accurately and still miss who is carrying it. That is the gap this project is
trying to help close — which is why the next thing on this site is an invitation
to tell us what happened to you.
