> A sinking feeling is what you feel first: I’ve had this before and oh no, it’s happening again.

## The rollercoaster

Initial hope, then despair, then encouragement, and ultimately back down in the
“dumps.” That cycle — not any one low point in it — is what patients living with
resistant infections describe. It repeats, and the repetition is the part least
likely to appear anywhere in a chart.

> [My] dressing lasted for months, [but I was] scared it (AMR) would happen again.

The fear sat alongside real gratitude for the people treating her:

> [But it’s a] relief to have people who are specialists to do these dressings for you

## Appointments that move too fast

> It seems they have just decided that their priority is getting through patients after patients, but it leaves the patient feeling less than prepared to face what they need.

Norma’s physician often felt rushed during their consultations, without time to
explain what AMR is, what side effects she could expect, or what the treatment
options were. The comorbidities that came later arrived as a surprise — and
could have been raised earlier, if that conversation had happened.

What she asks for is not more time so much as being let in on the reasoning:

> Make it a priority to, if the doctor has concerns about a medication and is hovering between several options, [tell] the patient…that there are those concerns…he should let the patient in on it so that they know what will be possible outcomes.

She holds herself to part of it too:

> I wish I had an in-person consultation but I also wish that I had looked up some of these things myself

## “A superbug”

The word arrived from her own family. Her daughter put it this way:

> Mom, remember when we were kids and you were reading us all about the fact of overuse of antibiotics and [that] we’re gonna create a superbug? Mom, this is what you got — a superbug!

That word does damage on its own. People hear “superbug” and assume no
antibiotic will work at all. In reality clinicians usually still have options;
resistance makes them narrower and the treatment more complicated. And the
misconception underneath it is that the person has become resistant to
antibiotics. They haven’t; the bacteria have.

## The weight of the schedule

Repeat appointments, additional testing, specialist visits, longer treatment —
each one a journey, and each one a cost. Norma once collapsed from going to the
hospital so much.

## What helped

The church, and a friend who believes in the power of prayer, helped her greatly
through the isolated stretches. Nobody prescribed or arranged that; she found it
herself. Both she and Sunny Loo raised the same absence with us: there is no
community of AMR patients to connect with, and both said they would value one.

> We fail to address the whole person.
