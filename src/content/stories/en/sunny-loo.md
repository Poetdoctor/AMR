> I want others to understand how I feel and stop long enough to allow me to share how I feel instead of going straight into…the treatment

## The pause that doesn’t happen

The request is not for a different treatment. It is for the moment before it —
long enough to say how this is actually going, and to be heard saying it, before
the conversation moves on to the plan.

Asking is not free, either:

> I would appreciate [my physician] asking about how I feel…[but] they would send me straight to counseling if I did ask

The counselling that referral leads to would come out of his own pocket. So the
psychological part of a resistant infection gets raised, redirected, and quietly
priced out of reach in a single step.

## What the appointment leaves out

> Doctors [get straight to] what illness, what procedure, etc… but often this is a small part of a patient’s day to day concern…most patients require a psychological aspect, how do I go through the rest of the day with what I have, how do I get through tomorrow with what I have?

Clinicians we spoke to described the same gap from the other side. Dr. Anthony
Liu told us that as physicians move higher in their training they can lose the
basics of a condition, and debrief patients on it less as a result. Lesley
Kooner, an infection control nurse, extends that to nurses — the providers who
spend the most time at the bedside, and who are therefore best placed to explain
any of it.

## What helped

Online games with friends: an existing connection that carried on regardless of
what his body was doing, and that did not require him to be a patient in order
to take part. Like Norma Washburn, he raised the absence of any community of AMR
patients to connect with, and said he would value one.

> When we talk about our experience, we are just asking to be understood, to be supported.
